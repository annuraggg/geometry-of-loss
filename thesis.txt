The Historical Origins & Etymology of Gerrymandering
The term “gerrymander” originated in Massachusetts in 1812. That year Governor Elbridge Gerry (a Democratic-Republican) approved a redistricting plan to entrench his party’s power. A Boston newspaper cartoon by Elkanah Tisdale ridiculed one oddly-shaped new Essex County district as a salamander (complete with claws and tongue). The portmanteau “Gerry–mander” (Gerry + salamander) was coined in the Boston Gazette on March 26, 1812. (Despite the gerrymander, Gerry himself lost re-election – but the reshaped map secured his party a legislative majority.) The iconic 1812 cartoon (below) shows the creature-like district that gave the name to this practice.

Key Points
In 1812 Massachusetts, a state legislative redistricting produced a famously bizarre district. A Federalist‐leaning cartoon portrayed it as a salamander, coining “Gerrymander”.
The word was first used in print on March 26, 1812, by the Boston Gazette. Although Gerry signed the bill, it was his party’s plan – and he lost his own election – yet his party retained control of the legislature.
From this episode onward, gerrymandering has meant drawing electoral maps for political advantage, often by contorting geometry to alter outcomes.
The Mechanics of Distortion: Packing & Cracking
Modern partisan gerrymanders work by two complementary tricks: packing and cracking. In packing, map-drawers jam as many voters of the opposing party as possible into a small number of districts, “wasting” their votes on overwhelming wins. In cracking, the remaining opponents are spread thinly across many districts so that they narrowly lose each one. In practice this yields a telltale pattern: the packed party wins few seats by large margins, and the packers win many seats by slim majorities. The effect is that a party can win a large share of seats with only a modest share of the vote.

Packing: Concentrating opponents’ voters. Opposing-party voters are “crammed into a small number of districts,” giving them lopsided wins (all surplus votes beyond 50%+1 are wasted). The packed districts yield huge margins, but this “wastes” votes that could have helped win other seats.
Cracking: Spreading opponents across districts. The rest of the opposing party’s voters are divided among many districts so they form only a minority in each. In practice this means they lose those districts by narrow margins. The result is that the dominant party wins many more districts (often with small majorities) while the minority party wins only the packed seats.
Impact on Seats vs Votes: Together, packing and cracking create a wide gap between popular vote share and seat share. For example, after Wisconsin’s 2011 map (drawn by Republicans) the GOP won 60 of 99 Assembly seats (~61%) despite only 49% of the statewide vote; in 2014 they won 63 seats with 52% of the vote. As the Brennan Center notes, “in extreme cases” a party can win a majority of seats while actually losing the statewide vote. This structural distortion means the legislature’s composition can be skewed far beyond voters’ actual preferences.
Mathematical Metrics & Detection
Political scientists and courts have devised quantitative tests to detect such distortions. These include:

Efficiency Gap: Developed by Stephanopoulos & McGhee (2015), the efficiency gap counts wasted votes – votes beyond what was needed to win or all votes for losing candidates – for each party. The gap is the difference in wasted votes (divided by total votes). A large efficiency gap (e.g. >7–8%) indicates that one party’s voters are systematically packed/cracked, translating a modest vote advantage into many more seats.
Compactness Scores (Polsby–Popper & Reock): These geometric metrics test district shape. Polsby–Popper scores a district by comparing its area to the area of a circle with the same perimeter (score = 4π × Area/Perimeter²). A perfectly round district scores 1; convoluted, fractal boundaries score near 0. Reock scores by the ratio of the district’s area to the area of the smallest enclosing circle. If a district is nearly circular, its Reock score is near 1; if it is long and snake-like, the score is low. Unusually low average or minimum compactness across a map is a red flag for manipulation.
Seat–Vote Curve & Partisan Bias: One can plot the seat–vote curve – the seats a party would win as a function of its vote share. An unfair map will show a curve tilted to favor one side. A common summary is the partisan bias: the amount by which a party’s seat share at 50% vote differs from 50%. Formally, one “shifts” each district’s votes to simulate a 50–50 statewide tie and computes the seat shares. The deviation from 50–50 seats is partisan bias. For example, if under a hypothetical 50% vote a party would win 55% of seats, the plan has a 5% bias toward that party. A large bias indicates a gerrymandered advantage.
Reforms, Independent Commissions & Algorithmic Solutions
In response to gerrymandering, many jurisdictions are experimenting with reforms and computational tools:

Independent Redistricting Commissions: Some states have removed partisan map-drawers from the process. Voters in California (Prop. 11 in 2008, Prop. 20 in 2010) created a Citizens Redistricting Commission for congressional and state districts. Similar citizen commissions exist in Arizona, Iowa and elsewhere; in 2018 Colorado, Michigan, Missouri and Utah approved commissions by ballot initiative. Empirical analysis suggests these commissions can produce fairer maps. For instance, California’s commission maps were broadly fair to both parties and more competitive than the prior legislature-drawn maps. (Democrats gained a slight advantage under the CRC plan, but overall outcomes were within the national norm and substantially more balanced than previous maps.) Independent commissions typically have strict criteria (equal population, compactness, respect for communities, etc.) and public hearings to ensure transparency.

Algorithmic and Ensemble Methods: Modern computational approaches use algorithms to sample many possible maps. For example, Fifield et al. (2020) describe a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method that treats redistricting as a graph-partitioning problem; their algorithm can enforce contiguity and equal-population while generating random valid plans. In practice, one generates a large ensemble of maps (each meeting nonpartisan criteria) and compares the enacted plan’s partisan outcomes to the ensemble’s distribution. As Mattingly’s Quantifying Gerrymandering group explains, encoding desirable map criteria into an ensemble “lets the typical electoral behavior reveal itself”. If the real map’s result is a statistical outlier (e.g. Republican seat share far above the ensemble’s norm), this is strong evidence of gerrymandering. Such ensemble-based outlier tests have been used in legal cases (e.g. Wisconsin’s and Pennsylvania’s suits) to quantify how extreme a plan is relative to neutral alternatives.
